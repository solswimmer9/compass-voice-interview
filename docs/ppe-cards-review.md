# PPE cards for review

For Solomon. Last updated 12 November 2026. This sheet was produced once from the card files in `src/content/tutors/ppe/cards/` by a one-off script that is not kept in the repo, so on that day the text below matched what Prof Matt and the feedback model get. It can be edited by hand; if a card file changes, update its section here too.

Nine cards are approved and already reach students (listed at the end). The other 31 are drafts. Students never hear a draft and never get feedback from one; only tester accounts do. On 12 November every draft was brought up to the depth of the nine approved cards. They are all still drafts and none is marked as reviewed: nothing reaches students until you approve it.

## How to review

Each draft below has four parts:

- **Opening**: the heading. This is the question Prof Matt asks first.
- **Prof Matt sees**: the guidance the voice tutor gets. It names no academics or podcast hosts.
- **Background**: notes only the reasoning and feedback models see. These may name academics and papers.
- **What changed on 12 November**: so you can see what is new.

For each card, reply with its number and one of:

- **Approve**: it goes to students as written.
- **Change**: say what to change; it stays a draft until you have seen the new text.
- **Drop**: it is removed.

**Whoever records an approval in a card file must also reset its `recheckBy` date to twelve months after the approval date.** The drafts carry a provisional date of 2027-11-12.

Approving a card makes it available to students straight away. When a card is approved, its "recheck by" date (set on cards that rely on current affairs or recent research) will be moved to twelve months after the day you approve it.

Things to watch for as you read: anything that sounds like the tutor taking a side, a question that hands the student the answer, wording a professor would not say aloud, and any fact you are not sure is true.

### 1. When should a rational person change their mind?

`changing-your-mind` · philosophy · draft · leads on to: what-is-a-problem, practical-belief-freedom, distrust-the-messenger

**What changed on 12 November:** Added an everyday scaffold (a referee and a replay) and a check that the student revises the part of their view the challenge actually hit.

**Prof Matt sees** (90 words)

Hook: we admire people who change their minds for good reasons and distrust those who change them constantly. The tension: holding firm can be integrity or stubbornness; changing can be open-minded or spineless. Push on: what kind of reason should move you: new evidence, a better argument, or someone you respect disagreeing? How much weight should you give a person who is equally informed and disagrees? Ask the student to give their own favourite view its strongest objection and say whether it attacks their facts, their inference or their values.

**Background** (227 words)

From the What? Why? When? episode When Thinking Goes Wrong, whose brief description concerns failures of thinking, and pack question MWQ06. The pack's exercise: give your preferred policy its strongest objection and state whether it challenges facts, inference or values.

A strong student separates kinds of reason: evidence that changes the facts; arguments that expose a mistake in inference; value considerations they had not weighed. Changing your mind in proportion to the strength of the reason is the ideal; a single anecdote should move you less than systematic evidence.

If the student is stuck, scaffold with an everyday case: a referee shown a replay, or a doctor given a new test result. What should move them, and how far? Then hand the question back for a belief the student actually holds.

Peer disagreement: if someone equally informed and careful disagrees, that is itself some evidence you might be wrong; but giving it full weight would make everyone abandon their views. Good answers propose a middle path, such as lowering confidence and re-examining the reasoning.

This card also tests the student live: do they actually revise under challenge during the interview, and do they revise the precise part the challenge hit? Weak moves: "always keep an open mind" without criteria; treating disagreement as proof; changing position at the first push; refusing to move even when a point lands.

### 2. Should people today pay for climate damage caused before they were born?

`climate-debt-past-emissions` · philosophy · draft · leads on to: state-apology, climate-collective-action, climate-concern-polarised · recheck by 2027-11-12

**What changed on 12 November:** Already at the approved cards' depth; text unchanged. Recheck date added. See "please check" item 4.

**Prof Matt sees** (88 words)

Hook: much of the carbon in the atmosphere was emitted generations ago, mostly by countries that are now rich. The tension: today's citizens did nothing wrong, but they may still enjoy the wealth those emissions built. Push on: does inheriting benefits from past emissions create a duty, even without personal wrongdoing? If historical responsibility points to one set of countries and present ability to pay points to another, which should decide who compensates? Does what is politically feasible change what justice requires, or only what policy can deliver?

**Background** (186 words)

From Daniel Butt, "Immoderate Integrationism: History and Climate Justice", Erasmus Journal for Philosophy and Economics 18(2), 2026 (published 5 March 2026; some records give 2025). Butt responds to a backward-looking account of climate justice grounded in the polluter-pays principle, which focuses on historical responsibility while staying attentive to feasibility. He argues for a broader view that widens the scope of past wrongdoing counted and the range of actions rectification demands, while accepting that in an emergency any workable theory that reduces harm is valuable.

The pack's practice note: distinguish culpability, benefit, collective continuity and capacity; test whether feasibility changes what justice requires or only what policy can deliver.

Positions a student may reach: polluter pays (but the polluters are dead, and early emitters did not know the harm); beneficiary pays (those who inherited the gains owe repair); ability to pay (whoever can afford it should, regardless of history). Each picks out different countries and people.

Weak moves: assuming responsibility requires personal guilt; ignoring ignorance of harm in the past; conflating what is fair with what is achievable. The student is not expected to know the paper.

**Sources:** [Immoderate Integrationism: History and Climate Justice (2026)](https://ejpe.org/journal/article/view/1016); Solomon's PPE interview pack (11 November 2026): original practice questions

### 3. Should freedom of movement be defended as an economic benefit or as an individual right?

`free-movement-right` · philosophy · draft · leads on to: referendum-or-manifesto, power-and-authority, offensive-speech · recheck by 2027-11-12

**What changed on 12 November:** Already at the approved cards' depth; text unchanged. Recheck date added.

**Prof Matt sees** (100 words)

Hook: arguments about freedom of movement are often made in terms of growth and jobs, but it can also be defended as a person's liberty. The tension: an economic case can be overturned by evidence; a rights case does not depend on results. Push on: think about citizens who will never move abroad; does the right matter to them? Could a right remain justified if its economic consequences were negative? Widen it: what gives a state the right to control who enters at all, and do citizens and non-citizens have different moral claims? Keep your own view out of it.

**Background** (176 words)

Combines the pack's note on Political Currency episodes 336 and 338 (electoral risks of restoring free movement; its framing and reciprocal rights) with Williams pack questions MWQ09 (what gives a state the right to control entry?) and MWQ10 (should every nation have its own state?), from the episode Why Are There Countries?

A strong student separates three justifications: economic (aggregate gains, labour markets), rights-based (liberty to move, live and work), and reciprocal (a bargain between countries). Each responds differently to evidence: bad economic effects defeat the first but not obviously the second. Citizens who never move still gain the option and the reciprocal rights.

Borders: distinguish the historical explanation of a border from a moral defence of it, and a state from a nation. Arguments for control include self-determination, protecting public services and the shared institutions citizens built; arguments against include the arbitrariness of birthplace.

Weak moves: treating the economic and rights cases as one; asserting a right without saying who holds it and against whom; arguing from partisan conclusions. Assess reasoning only, never the conclusion.

### 4. Can an act be perfectly legal and still morally wrong?

`legal-but-wrong` · philosophy · draft · leads on to: power-and-authority, prepunishment, state-apology

**What changed on 12 November:** Added a small everyday scaffold and asks what the official should actually do.

**Prof Matt sees** (94 words)

Hook: a fictional spy is said to have a "licence to kill"; someone authorised it, but does that make the killing right? The tension: law is a set of rules people made; morality is a question about what we ought to do. Push on: separate three questions: who authorised the act, whether the law allows it, and whether it is morally permissible. What follows for an official ordered to do something legal but wrong? Why should the state have special permission to use force at all, and when does its force stop being legitimate?

**Background** (212 words)

From the What? Why? When? episode License to Kill, which uses a James Bond example to connect the state, law, policing and the justification of force; and pack questions MWQ03 and MWQ11. The pack's application: something can be legally authorised without being morally justified.

A strong student separates legal validity (made by the right process), authority (the right to command), and moral permissibility. Examples: historical laws that permitted grave injustice; everyday cases such as legal but cruel behaviour. If the student is stuck, start small: breaking a friend's confidence is usually legal; does that make it fine?

The official's dilemma tests whether the student thinks a legal order creates any moral reason to obey, perhaps because of the value of a working legal system, and where that reason runs out. A good answer says what the official should do, not only what they may do: resign, refuse, report, or comply under protest.

The state's use of force: a classic answer is that the state may use force because it does so under public rules, for public ends, and is accountable; legitimacy fails when force is arbitrary, unaccountable or used against the people it serves.

Weak moves: "legal means right" or "law is just opinion"; ignoring the official's position; asserting legitimacy without criteria.

**Sources:** [What? Why? When? (Jesus College Oxford): License to Kill (publisher description)](https://shows.acast.com/what-why-when/episodes/6874fc3cc81f4db04a4a9a3a); Solomon's PPE interview pack (11 November 2026): original practice questions

### 5. If a machine says it is in pain, how could you tell whether it is?

`machine-in-pain` · philosophy · draft · leads on to: practical-belief-freedom, changing-your-mind, rights-outweigh-benefit

**What changed on 12 November:** Added what the two changed conditions test and a late question on acting under uncertainty. Related cards replaced (concert tickets was a poor fit).

**Prof Matt sees** (98 words)

Hook: a chatbot tells you it is suffering and asks you not to switch it off. The tension: we only ever see other people's behaviour, never their feelings, yet we are confident other people feel pain. Push on: what is your evidence that your best friend feels pain? Does that same evidence apply to the machine, and if not, what is the difference? If the answer is "it is just following its programming", ask whether a brain is doing anything different. Change a condition: what if it were made of living cells, or never said anything but flinched?

**Background** (254 words)

An epistemology question in disguise: the problem of other minds. The student should notice that the evidence for anyone's pain is indirect (behaviour, reports, similarity of body and origin) and ask which of those the machine shares.

Lines a student may reach: argument from similarity (other humans are built like me, so they probably feel like me; the machine is not); behaviour is enough (if it acts in every way as if in pain, denying it is arbitrary); origin matters (it was designed to produce the words, so the words are not evidence). Each has a counter: similarity is a matter of degree (animals); behaviour can be faked (actors); human words are also produced by a mechanism.

The two changed conditions pull apart the evidence: living cells raise similarity of body without words; flinching gives behaviour without report. A strong student says which change moves them and why, and so finds out which kind of evidence they were really relying on.

A practical turn worth making late on: under uncertainty, what should we do? The cost of wrongly ignoring real suffering differs from the cost of wrongly being kind to a machine; a strong student can weigh the two without claiming to have settled the facts.

Weak moves: asserting "machines cannot feel" as a fact without reasons; treating the question as one about current technology only; using "conscious", "aware" and "intelligent" interchangeably.

Good push-ons: whether a fish or an insect is an easier or harder case; what single observation would change the student's mind.

### 6. Does the fact that we must treat people as responsible give us a reason to believe they are free?

`practical-belief-freedom` · philosophy · draft · leads on to: changing-your-mind, machine-in-pain, prepunishment · recheck by 2027-11-12

**What changed on 12 November:** Already at the approved cards' depth; text unchanged. Recheck date added.

**Prof Matt sees** (96 words)

Hook: we blame, praise and hold people to promises as if they choose freely, and it is hard to imagine living any other way. The tension: needing to believe something for practical purposes is not the same as having evidence it is true. Push on: does needing a belief to live make it justified, or even true? Ask for an everyday example of believing something because you must act on it. Could a rational being understand right and wrong without sharing our ideas of cause and effect? Keep technical words out unless the student uses them.

**Background** (201 words)

From Anil Gomes and Andrew Stephenson, "On the Practical Necessity of the Categories", Philosophy and Phenomenological Research 112(2), 2026 (online December 2025). In our own words: Kant says we cannot know whether all finite rational beings share our forms of sensibility; the paper asks whether we can settle on practical rather than theoretical grounds that they share our forms of understanding, sets out and evaluates such an argument, and connects it to Kant's views on morality, freedom and causation and the limits of our knowledge of the moral law.

The interview question abstracts the idea: practical reasons for accepting a belief versus evidence for its truth. The pack's practice note: distinguish evidence for truth from practical reasons for acceptance; explain technical claims in ordinary language.

A strong student separates truth, justification and usefulness. Examples: trusting a friend, assuming the future will resemble the past, believing you can succeed. Some argue that where evidence cannot settle a question and we must act, practical reasons may legitimately tip belief; others say that is wishful thinking.

Weak moves: confusing "useful" with "true"; assuming free will is obviously real or obviously illusory; using jargon. The student is not expected to know Kant or the paper.

**Sources:** [On the Practical Necessity of the Categories (2026)](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/phpr.70080); Solomon's PPE interview pack (11 November 2026): original practice questions

### 7. Do you have to keep a promise to someone who has died?

`promise-to-the-dead` · philosophy · draft · leads on to: telling-the-truth, state-apology, climate-debt-past-emissions

**What changed on 12 November:** Spoken text shortened. Added why the "she would never have found out" case is the sharpest test, and how to use the cost of the trip. Related cards replaced.

**Prof Matt sees** (110 words)

Hook: you promised your grandmother you would scatter her ashes on a hill she loved. She has died, nobody else knows about the promise, and the trip is expensive. The tension: a promise seems to be owed to a person, but she can no longer be disappointed or harmed. Push on: who exactly is wronged if you break it? Does a promise bind because of what breaking it does to the other person, or because of what it says about you? Change a condition: what if she would never have found out even while alive? If the answer is "it keeps you trustworthy", is that still a duty to her?

**Background** (289 words)

The core question is what grounds a promissory obligation. Three rough answers a student may reach in their own words: (1) promises matter because breaking them harms or disappoints the promisee; (2) promises matter because they protect a valuable practice of trust; (3) making a promise hands the other person a kind of authority over what you do, which may not end at death.

If (1), the duty seems to vanish at death, which many find counterintuitive; a strong student tests whether the dead can be harmed at all, for example by having their wishes ignored or their reputation damaged. If (2), the duty survives, but it becomes a duty to everyone rather than to the grandmother, and secret breaches seem harmless to the practice. If (3), the student must explain who now holds that authority, and whether anyone can release you.

The "never found out while alive" condition is the sharpest test: if breaking a secret promise to a living person still seems wrong, then disappointment was never the whole story, and (1) is in trouble.

The cost of the trip is there to test strength, not existence: a student may say the duty is real but can be outweighed. Ask what would outweigh it and whether the grandmother's own likely wishes matter.

Weak moves to watch: asserting "you just should" as if a value were a fact; sliding between "wrong" and "bad for you"; a both-sides summary without choosing a ground; changing what "promise" means mid-argument (a contract, a hope, an intention).

Good push-ons: secret versus public promises; a promise that has become very costly; a promise the person would now release you from; whether wills are a kind of promise to the dead that we enforce.

### 8. Why should one person's right sometimes outweigh a larger benefit to other people?

`rights-outweigh-benefit` · philosophy · draft · leads on to: organ-allocation, prepunishment, offensive-speech · recheck by 2027-11-12

**What changed on 12 November:** Already at the approved cards' depth; text unchanged. Recheck date added.

**Prof Matt sees** (98 words)

Hook: we would not take one person's kidney to save two others, even though more lives would be saved. The tension: if outcomes are what matter, rights look like an obstacle; if rights matter, we need to say why numbers sometimes do not. Push on: is it the difference between saving lives and merely giving people pleasure that explains our reactions, or do rights add something extra? Try a case where a small harm to one person would give great enjoyment to a huge crowd. Can welfare play a central role in morality without every right becoming tradeable?

**Background** (186 words)

From Thomas Sinclair, "Rights, conflicts, and the mechanics of claims", Journal of Moral Philosophy, 2024. In our own words: a theory of rights must explain why some interests get special weight in deciding what is permissible while others do not. Sinclair examines Alec Walen's account of claims and argues that its welfare principle gives welfare interests too much priority, undermining its appeal; he considers repairs but finds deeper questions unresolved.

A strong student contrasts aggregation (add up benefits and harms) with constraints (some things may not be done to a person whatever the total). Classic cases: transplant, a crowd watching a painful broadcast, a trolley. They should notice that intuitions differ between saving lives and providing minor benefits, and ask whether that is because of rights or because small benefits should not be added up against serious harms.

The final push-on tests consistency: if welfare grounds rights, enough welfare might seem to override them; a good answer explains which interests are protected and why.

Weak moves: "rights just exist"; counting only numbers; treating all trade-offs as alike. The student is not expected to know the paper.

**Sources:** [Rights, conflicts, and the mechanics of claims (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1163/17455243-20234343); Solomon's PPE interview pack (11 November 2026): original practice questions

### 9. Can a state owe an apology for wrongs committed before its citizens were born?

`state-apology` · philosophy · draft · leads on to: climate-debt-past-emissions, promise-to-the-dead, legal-but-wrong · recheck by 2027-11-12

**What changed on 12 November:** Hook made more general; new changed condition on whether it is still the same state. Recheck date added.

**Prof Matt sees** (93 words)

Hook: states and other institutions sometimes apologise for wrongs done long ago, such as slavery. The tension: no one alive committed the wrong, yet the state as an institution continues. Push on: separate individual guilt from continuing institutional responsibility. If a company that did harm a century ago is still trading, does it still owe something? Would an apology without any material repair meet the obligation? Do acknowledgement, compensation and reform answer different kinds of wrong? Change a condition: what if the state's borders and system of government have changed completely since then?

**Background** (203 words)

From the pack's note on Political Currency episode 337, whose description includes royal expressions of sorrow over slavery, and the pack's theme on historical responsibility: state apologies raise a distinction between personal guilt and institutional obligations across generations; test whether acknowledgement, compensation and institutional reform address different kinds of wrong.

Positions: (1) only individuals can be guilty, so no one now owes anything; (2) institutions persist and can bear obligations, like a company or a university; (3) present citizens inherit benefits from past wrongs, which may ground a duty even without guilt. The pack pairs this with Daniel Butt's work on historical responsibility and climate justice (see the climate-debt card).

A strong student distinguishes kinds of response: an apology expresses acknowledgement; compensation repairs loss; reform prevents repetition. Each may be owed for different reasons, and to different people.

The changed condition tests what makes it the same state: continuity of law, institutions, territory or people. If little of that continues, position (2) weakens and the student must lean on (3) or give up the claim.

Weak moves: assuming responsibility requires personal guilt without saying why; ignoring the company analogy; treating apology as costless or as worthless; arguing from political conclusions. Assess reasoning only.

### 10. Is it ever wrong to tell the truth?

`telling-the-truth` · philosophy · draft · leads on to: promise-to-the-dead, offensive-speech, distrust-the-messenger

**What changed on 12 November:** Uses the "they perform tomorrow" detail and separates morally wrong, unkind and unwise. Related cards replaced.

**Prof Matt sees** (102 words)

Hook: a friend asks whether you liked the song they wrote; it was bad, and they perform it tomorrow. The tension: honesty seems valuable in itself, yet a true statement can cause harm and achieve nothing. Push on: is there a difference between lying and simply not saying something? If telling the truth can be wrong, what makes it wrong: the harm, the timing or the motive? Ask the student to state their principle in one sentence, then test it on a harder case, such as a painful truth someone asked for. What is "wrong" doing here: morally wrong, unkind, or unwise?

**Background** (262 words)

Tests whether the student can separate questions that are easily run together: whether lying is wrong, whether withholding is wrong, and whether saying something true can be wrong. A good answer draws distinctions: lying versus omission versus tactful framing; the content of a statement versus the act of stating it at a given time and to a given audience.

Positions a student might build: consequence-focused (telling the truth is wrong when it causes avoidable harm and no compensating good); duty-focused (honesty is a duty but kindness is another, and they can conflict); character-focused (the question is what an honest and kind person would do).

The song case has a detail worth using: the performance is tomorrow. A truth that cannot help may differ from one that can, so ask whether the answer changes if the performance were in a month. The friend also asked, which matters for whether withholding is a kind of deception.

The last push-on separates kinds of wrong. A student who says "it would be wrong" may mean only that it would be unkind or unwise; ask whether unkindness is a moral failing or something else.

Weak moves: "it depends" with no account of what it depends on; treating any true statement as automatically honest; ignoring that the person asked; failing to notice that a principle like "never cause harm" would forbid many true and important statements, such as a medical diagnosis.

Good push-ons: a truth told to hurt someone; a truth the listener has a right to; whether cruelty makes a true statement less honest or just worse.

### 11. Can we call something a social problem without first making a value judgement?

`what-is-a-problem` · philosophy · draft · leads on to: rough-sleeping, lower-house-prices, changing-your-mind

**What changed on 12 November:** Added a congestion scaffold and how each description of the housing problem picks its own remedy.

**Prof Matt sees** (91 words)

Hook: poverty, traffic jams and loneliness are all called problems, but a problem is always a problem by some standard. The tension: describing the facts seems neutral, yet calling them a problem implies something ought to be different. Push on: try poverty, congestion and loneliness as contrasting cases. Should rich countries pay for cuts in poorer countries, and is that an efficiency argument or a fairness argument? Why might elected leaders underinvest in climate protection at all?

**Background** (253 words)

From the What? Why? When? episode What can be done for Climate Change?, whose description identifies denial, economic costs, collective-action problems and obligations between generations; and pack questions MWQ12, MWQ15 and MWQ16. The pack's exercise: make a two-country emissions game, explain why individually attractive decisions produce a collectively damaging outcome, then test a treaty.

Structure: each country bears the full cost of its own cuts but gets only a share of the benefit, so free-riding is tempting (a prisoner's dilemma). A treaty must change payoffs: monitoring, penalties, side payments, or linking climate to trade.

Simple numbers the tutor can offer if the student wants them, saying they are made up: cutting costs a country 3; each country's cut gives 2 to every country. If I cut, I pay 3 and get 2 back, so I lose 1 whatever you do. If we both cut, each of us pays 3 and gets 4, a gain of 1. So each does better by not cutting, yet both do better if both cut. Ask the student what a treaty must change in those numbers.

Rich countries financing cuts in poorer ones: efficiency (cuts may be cheaper there) differs from fairness (historical emissions, ability to pay). The pack links this to the climate-justice and politicisation papers.

Elected leaders: costs now, benefits later and spread across the world; voters today bear the costs; future generations do not vote.

Weak moves: "countries are selfish" as a full explanation; ignoring that cooperation does sometimes happen; confusing moral disagreement with free-riding.

**Sources:** [What? Why? When? (Jesus College Oxford): What can be done for Climate Change? (publisher description)](https://shows.acast.com/what-why-when/episodes/6818c137609de352788a6312); Solomon's PPE interview pack (11 November 2026): original practice questions

### 25. Should a government try to lower house prices if that would make most homeowners poorer?

`lower-house-prices` · economics · draft · leads on to: infrastructure-veto, housing-water-pollution, rough-sleeping · recheck by 2027-11-12

**What changed on 12 November:** "Most voters own homes" softened to "many voters own their homes". Recheck date added.

**Prof Matt sees** (97 words)

Hook: high house prices lock many young people out, but many voters own their homes and much of their wealth is tied up in them. The tension: what helps future buyers may hurt present owners, and owners vote. Push on: how should the interests of present owners be weighed against those of future residents who are not yet here? Would building more homes fix the problem on its own, or do taxes, mortgage lending and how homes are owned and rented also need to change? What would the student measure to know if a policy had worked?

**Background** (187 words)

From Ben Ansell and co-authors, "Six provocations on the origins and impacts of the UK housing emergency", Journal of the British Academy 13(2), 2025. In our own words: British housing faces rising costs, declining choice, affordability stress and unmet need, with increasingly polarised outcomes affecting intergenerational conflict, inequality and sustainability; there is no single explanation or quick fix. The pack includes Ansell as a bonus housing researcher, not as a verified current undergraduate PPE tutor.

A strong student separates the distribution question (owners versus renters and future buyers; old versus young) from the mechanism question (supply, credit, interest rates, tax treatment of housing, tenure). Falling prices transfer wealth from owners to buyers; they may also cause negative equity and reduce spending.

Politics: homeowners are a large voting bloc, so governments have incentives to avoid falling prices; future residents have no vote in local planning. Building alone may not lower prices much if demand is driven by credit and tax; a good answer considers combinations.

Weak moves: assuming lower prices are good for everyone; one-cause explanations; ignoring who votes. The student is not expected to know the paper.

### 26. Should museums be free to enter?

`museum-entry-free` · economics · draft · leads on to: bag-charge, ticket-prices-fair, fairness-three-principles

**What changed on 12 November:** Added simple made-up numbers for average versus marginal cost, a note that no visitor figures are expected, and a third related card.

**Prof Matt sees** (91 words)

Hook: many national museums in Britain are free to enter, while plenty of other museums charge. The tension: if entry is free, everyone pays through taxes, including people who never visit. Push on: what does one extra visitor actually cost the museum, compared with the cost of running it? Who do you think visits free museums most, and does that matter to the argument? If a museum starts charging, who stops coming? Ask the student to separate what is efficient from what is fair, and say which one they are answering.

**Background** (281 words)

A card on average versus marginal cost and on who pays. The average cost per visitor (total running cost divided by visitors) can be high, but the marginal cost of one extra visitor when the museum is not crowded is close to zero. That is the efficiency case for free entry: charging turns away people who would gain something at almost no cost to anyone. Once crowded, the marginal cost rises, and timed tickets or charges start to make sense.

Simple numbers if the student needs them, said aloud as made-up numbers: a museum costs £10 million a year to run and has a million visitors, so the average cost is £10 a visit; an extra visitor on a quiet Tuesday costs almost nothing. Ask which number should set the price.

The fairness question is separate. Free entry funded by taxes means non-visitors pay; if visitors are mostly better-off or live nearby, that may be a transfer from poorer to richer or from the regions to big cities. But charging may deter exactly the visitors the museum most wants to reach. A strong student holds both and reaches a position, perhaps a targeted design (free for some, a charge for exhibitions, a charge for overseas visitors) and says what it gives up.

The "who visits" push-on asks for the student's guess and then how they would find out; they are not expected to know any figures.

Weak moves: "culture should be free" as if a value settled the funding question; treating "cost" as one number; ignoring who actually visits.

Good push-ons: should swimming pools or buses be free by the same logic; what evidence would show whether free entry widened access.

### 27. A city caps rents at today's level. What happens the day after?

`rent-cap-day-after` · economics · draft · leads on to: ticket-prices-fair, lower-house-prices, rough-sleeping

**What changed on 12 November:** Spoken text shortened. Added simple numbers and the insiders-versus-newcomers point; removed a vague claim that real-world evidence is "mixed". The spoken text no longer gives away the shortage, and Background now prepares for a student who says a cap at today's level changes nothing on day one.

**Prof Matt sees** (90 words)

Hook: rents in a city have risen fast, so the council freezes them at today's prices. The tension: the cap helps tenants who already have a home, but it also changes what landlords and future tenants will do. Push on: start with the people living in the flats today; then ask what a landlord does when deciding whether to rent out, sell or repair a flat. Who gains and who loses, and are they the same people? A year on, how does someone new to the city find a flat?

**Background** (351 words)

A change-the-condition incentives card for students with or without economics. A strong student may say that on the day after, nothing has changed yet: a cap at today's level does not bind until market rents would have risen. Credit that, then ask what happens as rents elsewhere rise, and whether landlords and tenants change what they do sooner because they expect the cap to bite. The first-round effect, once it binds, is that sitting tenants pay less. Second-round effects the student should reach by reasoning: if the capped rent is below what people would pay, more people want flats than are available (a shortage); something other than price decides who gets them, such as queues, connections or side payments; landlords may spend less on repairs, convert to short lets or sell; fewer new flats may be built, unless new builds are exempt.

Simple numbers if useful, said aloud as made-up numbers: 100 flats, 150 households who would pay the capped rent. Fifty miss out. Ask how they are chosen and who is likely to win that contest.

A strong student also weighs the other side: rent rises can force people out of their communities; tenants have little bargaining power; moving is costly, which gives a landlord power over a sitting tenant. A cap can be designed (limits on yearly increases rather than a freeze, exemptions for new builds) to reduce the side effects. The student is not expected to know evidence from any real city; if they cite some, ask what comparison it rests on.

The "who gains" push-on separates insiders from outsiders: the long-term tenant gains most; the newcomer, often younger or poorer, may lose. A strong student notices that a policy aimed at the vulnerable can help some vulnerable people at the expense of others.

Weak moves: stopping at the first-round effect; assuming landlords or tenants are all alike; asserting "housing is a right" as if it settled the policy question (a value is not a mechanism); assuming the market price is automatically fair.

Good push-ons: what you would need to measure to judge the policy after five years.

### 28. Should a state give up some cultural uniformity in schools if that gets more children to attend?

`resisting-education` · economics · draft · leads on to: schooling-for-marriage, democracy-and-growth, political-myth · recheck by 2027-11-12

**What changed on 12 November:** New changed condition on the language of teaching. Recheck date added.

**Prof Matt sees** (103 words)

Hook: schools teach skills, but they also pass on a culture, and some groups feel that culture is not theirs. The tension: a common curriculum can create equal citizenship, or it can feel like being made to assimilate. Push on: when does each happen? If a group responds by leaving school or setting up its own, is pushing harder likely to work? What evidence would tell cultural resistance apart from a simple lack of resources such as money, teachers or transport? Change a condition: if families objected to the language lessons are taught in, rather than what is taught, would the answer change?

**Background** (187 words)

From Jean-Paul Carvalho, Mark Koyama and Cole Williams, "Resisting Education", Journal of the European Economic Association 22(6), 2024. In our own words: schooling changes cultural identities as well as skills; their theoretical model explains how groups that feel culturally excluded may resist by leaving school, countering its messages, or building alternative institutions, so imposing one culture can backfire; they link these responses to persistent economic disadvantage and suggest moderating cultural content may prevent a backlash.

A strong student sees the trade-off: shared civic education has value (common language, equal opportunity, shared rights), but if it drives some families away, those children may end up with less education and less integration. The question is which goals are worth some loss of the other.

Evidence: resistance would show up as low attendance even where schools are free and close, attendance responding to curriculum changes, or growth of alternative schools; lack of resources would show up as attendance tracking cost, distance and teacher supply.

Weak moves: assuming culture in schools is neutral; treating resisting groups as irrational; ignoring the children's own interests. The pack pairs this with the democracy-and-growth card.

### 29. Why might a rich country fail to end rough sleeping?

`rough-sleeping` · economics · draft · leads on to: what-is-a-problem, lower-house-prices, welfare-cuts-fair · recheck by 2027-11-12

**What changed on 12 November:** New push-on: who is responsible when several services each hold part of the answer. Recheck date added.

**Prof Matt sees** (94 words)

Hook: a wealthy country could afford to house everyone sleeping on its streets, yet rough sleeping persists. The tension: is this a shortage of homes, of money, or of coordination between services? Push on: separate housing availability, income insecurity and support services such as health and addiction care. When several services each hold part of the answer, who is responsible if nobody solves it? What evidence would tell a supply problem apart from a coordination problem? Change a condition: if every rough sleeper were offered a flat tomorrow, what would happen a year later?

**Background** (219 words)

From the pack's note on Political Currency episode 336, whose description touches on ending rough sleeping and describes the housing discussion as complex without settling on a prescription.

A strong student separates causes: flows into homelessness (evictions, leaving prison or care, relationship breakdown), barriers to leaving it (lack of affordable lets, benefit rules, mental health and addiction), and the institutional problem that several agencies each hold part of the solution and none owns the outcome. Different causes imply different remedies: building homes, raising incomes, or joining up services.

The responsibility push-on: when several bodies each own part of a problem, each can say it did its part. A strong student sees that a problem can persist without anyone choosing it, and asks what change would give someone ownership of the outcome.

Evidence: if places with similar housing costs have very different rates, coordination or support likely matters; if rates track rents closely, supply likely matters. The "flat tomorrow" thought experiment exposes whether support is needed to sustain a tenancy, and whether new people would keep arriving on the streets.

Weak moves: assuming a single cause; treating rough sleepers as one uniform group; asserting "it is a choice by government" without saying what choice and why it is hard; ignoring the difference between ending it once and keeping it ended.

### 30. If parents keep daughters in school mainly to improve their marriage prospects, is the extra schooling a success?

`schooling-for-marriage` · economics · draft · leads on to: resisting-education, democracy-and-growth, what-is-a-problem · recheck by 2027-11-12

**What changed on 12 November:** Already at the approved cards' depth; text unchanged. Recheck date added. See "please check" item 3.

**Prof Matt sees** (103 words)

Hook: in one study of families in rural India, a model built on parents' answers suggested that if education stopped improving a daughter's marriage prospects, far fewer girls would still be in school at sixteen. The tension: more schooling is usually counted as a win, but the motive here is not the girl's own choice. Push on: how would we tell parents' beliefs about the returns to education apart from how much they value education for its own sake? Could a policy that changes marriage expectations increase schooling while reducing girls' autonomy? Is a model's "what if" the same as an observed result?

**Background** (203 words)

From Alison Andrew and Abi Adams, "Revealed Beliefs and the Marriage Market Return to Education", Quarterly Journal of Economics 140(3), 2025. In our own words: they develop a way to estimate subjective beliefs from hypothetical-choice surveys by varying the information given about future outcomes, and apply it to Rajasthani parents' beliefs about girls' marriage age, education and match quality. In their model, removing the perceived marriage-market return to education cuts the number of girls still in school at 16 by about 60%, with almost none continuing to 18; their survey responses accurately predicted actual schooling five years later. The pack notes the 60% figure is a model simulation, not the result of an experiment.

The pack's practice: separate outcomes, motives and agency; distinguish model-based counterfactuals from observed effects.

A strong student asks what "success" means: outcomes (more years in school, more skills) versus agency (the girl's own goals). Education might empower a girl later even if chosen for marriage reasons. Beliefs versus preferences: two families may both keep a daughter in school for different reasons; varying information is a way to tell them apart.

Weak moves: judging parents' motives harshly without understanding constraints; treating a simulation as observed fact; ignoring the girl's perspective.

### 31. Is it unfair to sell concert tickets to whoever will pay the most?

`ticket-prices-fair` · economics · draft · leads on to: rent-cap-day-after, organ-allocation, fairness-three-principles

**What changed on 12 November:** Scenario now clearly imaginary ("imagine"); added simple numbers and who keeps the extra money under each method. Related cards replaced.

**Prof Matt sees** (105 words)

Hook: imagine a band sells tickets at a low fixed price, they sell out in minutes, and resellers sell them on for five times as much. The tension: the band chose a low price to be fair to fans, but the tickets went to whoever paid most anyway. Push on: what does "fair" mean here: equal chances, rewarding the biggest fans, or letting people pay what it is worth to them? Is a queue or a lottery fairer than a price, and fairer for whom? Who gets the extra money in each version? Ask the student to defend their fairest method against its best objection.

**Background** (251 words)

A fairness-and-allocation card. When demand exceeds supply at the set price, something must ration: price, queueing time, luck or connections. Each has winners and losers. Price favours those with more money or who value it more; queues favour those with free time; lotteries give equal chances but ignore how much people care. The low face price does not stop high prices; it moves the extra money from the band to resellers.

A strong student separates the efficiency question (who values the ticket most) from the fairness question (whether willingness to pay reflects how much someone values it or just how rich they are), and notices that "fair" is doing different jobs in different arguments.

Simple numbers if useful, said aloud as made-up numbers: 1,000 seats, 5,000 fans who would pay the face price. Four in five miss out whatever the method; the question is only which ones.

The "who gets the money" push-on often surprises students: under a fixed low price with resale, the reseller keeps the gain; under a high price, the band does; under a lottery with non-transferable tickets, the lucky fan does. A strong student says which of these they mind and why.

Weak moves: treating "fair" as obvious; assuming high prices are always exploitative or always efficient; ignoring the resale market; a both-sides summary without choosing a method.

Good push-ons: banning resale (what happens next); named non-transferable tickets; whether the same logic applies to scarce things like hospital beds or university places, and why it might not.
