# PPE cards for review (selection)

Nine of the 40 PPE cards, three per strand, in full. These nine were approved by Solomon Allen on 2026-11-11; the other 31 are drafts. Generated from `src/content/tutors/ppe/cards/`. PPE is still limited to tester accounts.

For each card: **Opening** is what Prof Matt asks first. **Prof Matt sees** is the text given to the voice tutor. **Background** goes only to the reasoning and feedback models. Reply with the card number and your comment; to change a card, edit its file, named after the id.

## 1. Is fairness about equal chances, equal outcomes or rewarding contribution?

`fairness-three-principles` · philosophy · leads on to: welfare-cuts-fair, budget-doing-little, organ-allocation

**Prof Matt sees** (100 words)

Hook: politicians of every party claim their policies are fair, and they often mean different things by it. The tension: equal chances, equal outcomes and rewarding contribution sound compatible until a real policy forces a choice. Push on: ask the student to build one case where the three principles recommend different policies, then say which wins and why. Can someone deserve an advantage that came from circumstances they did not control, such as talent or a stable home? If they reach for "equal chances", ask what counts as a chance being equal. Watch for the meaning of "fair" shifting mid-answer.

**Background** (182 words)

From the pack's note on Political Currency episode 226 (George Osborne and Ed Balls), whose description brings together competing political meanings of fairness, messenger effects and adult skills. The pack treats it as a lens for the 2026 welfare, Budget and infrastructure discussions.

Three rough conceptions a student may separate: equality of opportunity (fair starting points, unequal results allowed); equality of outcome (results themselves should be closer); desert or contribution (people should get what they earn or put in). Need is a fourth the student may add. A strong answer builds a concrete case where they diverge, for example a university place, a tax change or a bonus pool, and defends a priority rather than listing all three.

The desert push-on is the hard part: talent, health and upbringing are unchosen, so rewarding contribution may reward luck. A good student either accepts some luck is fine to reward, with reasons, or explains what part of contribution is genuinely chosen.

Weak moves: asserting one meaning as obvious; changing the meaning of "fair" mid-argument; a both-sides summary; treating equal opportunity as easy to define.

**Sources:** [Political Currency, episode 226: What does 'fairness' actually mean in politics? (publisher description)](https://shows.acast.com/politicalcurrency-1/episodes/226); Solomon's PPE interview pack (11 November 2026): original practice questions

## 2. Who should receive a life-saving kidney when there are not enough to go round?

`organ-allocation` · philosophy · leads on to: fairness-three-principles, rights-outweigh-benefit, ticket-prices-fair

**Prof Matt sees** (102 words)

Hook: one kidney, several patients who need it. The tension: we could maximise the years of life saved, give everyone an equal chance, or prioritise whoever is worst off, and these point to different patients. Push on: ask what each principle values and where it clashes with another. Should organs go to whoever will gain most expected life-years? What if the patient with fewer expected years is much worse off? Is a lottery fair when patients have unequal chances of benefiting? Does fairness concern the procedure, the outcome, or both? Could willingness to pay ever be a fair way to allocate healthcare?

**Background** (165 words)

From the What? Why? When? episode Medical Ethics (Jesus College Oxford, hosted by Matt Williams), which discusses organ allocation using consequentialist, duty-based and virtue-ethical approaches, and the pack's questions MWQ01, MWQ02 and MWQ18. The pack's exercise: compare equal chances, greatest expected medical benefit and priority to the worst off.

Maximising expected life-years treats every year as equal, which favours younger and healthier recipients and can disadvantage the already unlucky. Equal chances (a lottery) respects each person equally but ignores how much good the organ will do. Priority to the worst off focuses on need but may waste scarce organs on poor matches.

Procedure versus outcome: a lottery can be fair as a procedure even if outcomes are unequal; a student should notice this distinction. Willingness to pay: separates efficiency from fairness, since ability to pay reflects income as well as need.

Weak moves: "it depends on the case" without a principle; treating age or lifestyle as obviously relevant or irrelevant; ignoring that someone must lose.

**Sources:** [What? Why? When? (Jesus College Oxford): Medical Ethics (publisher description)](https://shows.acast.com/what-why-when/episodes/686be4a7adc6a61ba8e5d1a0); Solomon's PPE interview pack (11 November 2026): original practice questions

## 3. If a perfectly reliable machine predicted a murder tomorrow, could it be just to punish the person today?

`prepunishment` · philosophy · leads on to: legal-but-wrong, rights-outweigh-benefit, machine-in-pain

**Prof Matt sees** (82 words)

Hook: imagine a machine that never gets predictions wrong says someone will commit murder tomorrow. The tension: punishment seems to require that someone has done something, yet waiting means a death we could have prevented. Push on: if punishing them today prevents the murder, the prediction was false; does that make the case incoherent? Is locking someone up to prevent harm morally different from punishing them, even if they lose the same liberty? Separate prevention from deserved punishment, and certainty from probability.

**Background** (196 words)

From Alex Kaiserman, "What's Wrong with Prepunishment?", Pacific Philosophical Quarterly 104(3), 2023. In our own words: the paper asks whether punishing someone before they commit a crime is necessarily wrong, testing objections from knowledge, desert, deterrence, time and duty. It separates principle from practice: the conditions that could justify prepunishment are extremely unlikely ever to hold, but advance punishment need not be wrong in every conceivable case.

The pack's practice note: separate prevention from deserved punishment, and certainty from probability; examine the consistency of the hypothetical.

A strong student spots the self-defeating structure: if punishment stops the act, the prediction fails, so the machine cannot be perfectly reliable about acts that punishment prevents. They may then reformulate (the machine predicts what you would do unless stopped). Desert: can you deserve punishment for something you have not yet done? Preventive detention (as with dangerous people who have not offended) shows we already accept some loss of liberty without guilt, which the student should notice.

Weak moves: "you can't punish someone for something they haven't done" with no reason; treating prediction and certainty as the same; ignoring preventive detention. The student is not expected to know the paper.

**Sources:** [What's Wrong with Prepunishment? (2023)](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/papq.12435); Solomon's PPE interview pack (11 November 2026): original practice questions

## 4. Should communities be able to veto infrastructure whose benefits are national?

`infrastructure-veto` · politics · leads on to: housing-water-pollution, lower-house-prices, what-is-a-problem

**Prof Matt sees** (93 words)

Hook: a data centre or a housing estate may help the whole country while one village bears the noise, traffic or lost fields. The tension: the gains are spread thinly over many people, the costs land heavily on a few. Push on: separate the size of the total benefit from who bears the local cost. Would paying compensation make a veto unnecessary, or would it make consent less meaningful? Should local residents be able to block housing for people who do not live there yet? Whose interests count, and through what decision procedure?

**Background** (189 words)

Combines the pack's note on Political Currency episode 338 (public acceptance of data centres) and the Williams pack question MWQ20 on local residents blocking housing. The pack's infrastructure theme: data centres are a strong case for national gains and local burdens, connecting to housing debates about who decides, who pays, who benefits, and whether compensation changes the moral claim to refuse development. The pack pairs it with Niclas Moneke's paper on nutrient neutrality and housing supply (see the housing-water-pollution card).

A strong student identifies the structure: concentrated costs and diffuse benefits create strong incentives for local opposition, so a local veto can block projects that would be worth it overall. But local people have special claims: they live there and the costs to them are real.

Compensation: if harm can be fully compensated, a veto looks like a hold-out; but if some losses are not tradeable, paying people may feel like buying consent. Future residents of new housing are absent from local decisions, an important point about whose interests count.

Weak moves: treating either "democracy" or "growth" as settling it; forgetting future residents; ignoring incentives to overstate local costs.

**Sources:** [Political Currency, episode 338: Regrets, leaks and data centres (publisher description)](https://shows.acast.com/politicalcurrency-1/episodes/emqs-regrets-leaks-and-data-centres); [What? Why? When? (Jesus College Oxford): What's the Problem? (publisher description)](https://shows.acast.com/what-why-when/episodes/69b9644505ad897c0293c30b); Solomon's PPE interview pack (11 November 2026): original practice questions

## 5. Can a political myth help people understand the truth?

`political-myth` · politics · leads on to: distrust-the-messenger, state-apology, free-movement-right

**Prof Matt sees** (81 words)

Hook: nations tell founding stories; movements tell stories about who they are and what they fight for. The tension: stories can bind people together and make sense of the world, but they can also hide inconvenient facts. Push on: how would the student tell a useful shared story apart from propaganda? Can a story that is not literally true still reveal something true? If a story unites most citizens but leaves out a minority, what should decide whether it is legitimate?

**Background** (185 words)

From Sophie Smith, "Poetry, myth and storytelling in the history of political theory", Critical Review of International Social and Political Philosophy 28(2), 2025 (online 2023). In our own words: the essay examines how myth, poetry and storytelling shape political theory, how myth should be distinguished from other narration, and whether it can reveal as well as conceal truths; treating mythmaking as central to the history of political ideas also brings forward feminist thinkers of the 1960s and 1970s who challenged dominant social myths.

A strong student separates literal truth from insight (a story can be false in detail but capture a real experience), and asks about function: does the story invite scrutiny or forbid it? Propaganda may be defined by intent to manipulate, by suppression of alternatives, or by indifference to truth.

Exclusion: a unifying story that erases a group may make that group's equal membership harder; challenging dominant myths can be a political act in its own right.

Weak moves: "myths are just lies"; treating all shared stories as propaganda; ignoring who tells the story. The student is not expected to know the paper.

**Sources:** [Poetry, myth and storytelling in the history of political theory (2023 online; 2025 issue)](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/13698230.2023.2248811); Solomon's PPE interview pack (11 November 2026): original practice questions

## 6. Would making neighbourhoods more politically mixed reduce hostility between political groups?

`mixed-neighbourhoods` · politics · leads on to: testing-election-causes, climate-concern-polarised, distrust-the-messenger

**Prof Matt sees** (90 words)

Hook: people increasingly dislike those who vote the other way, and one popular explanation is that we live surrounded by people who agree with us. The tension: one large British study found people can tell how politically uniform their area is, but living in such an area is not linked to disliking the other side more. Push on: does the absence of a link prove neighbourhoods have no causal effect? What else could hide an effect? Could online networks matter more than neighbours, and how would the student test that?

**Background** (199 words)

From James Tilley and Sara B. Hobolt, "The effect of politically homogenous neighbourhoods on affective polarization: Evidence from Britain", European Journal of Political Research 64(2), 2025 (online 2024). In our own words: using British data, they find people accurately perceive how politically homogeneous their area is, but neighbourhood homogeneity is not correlated with any measure of affective polarisation, robust to partisanship or Brexit identity, level of geography, length of residence and controls. They conclude geographical sorting is unlikely to be a major cause of affective polarisation.

The pack's practice: test the claim that no correlation means no causation. A strong student sees that zero correlation is strong evidence against a large effect, but an effect could be masked if, for example, people who move to like-minded areas were already less hostile, or effects offset each other. They might suggest tests: people who move for reasons unrelated to politics; changes over time.

Online networks: a rival explanation; testable by comparing heavy and light users, or natural experiments such as internet roll-outs.

Weak moves: assuming segregation must cause hostility; treating one study as final; confusing perception of the area with its effect. The student is not expected to know the study.

**Sources:** [The effect of politically homogenous neighbourhoods on affective polarization (2025)](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/1475-6765.12720); Solomon's PPE interview pack (11 November 2026): original practice questions

## 7. When, if ever, are welfare cuts fair?

`welfare-cuts-fair` · economics · leads on to: fairness-three-principles, budget-doing-little, rough-sleeping

**Prof Matt sees** (89 words)

Hook: governments looking for savings often turn to welfare, and every option creates winners and losers. The tension: a cut can save money and change incentives, but it lands on people who may already be struggling. Push on: separate the fiscal saving, the incentive effect, need, and the risk of hardship. Use simple numbers: would cutting everyone's payment by the same amount be fairer than cutting it by the same percentage? Then change the angle: why might a right-wing party oppose cutting welfare? What evidence would test that explanation?

**Background** (199 words)

From the pack's note on Political Currency episode 333, whose description covers whether the political costs of welfare cuts outweigh their benefits, with one host recalling resistance from the political right and the other stressing how hard reform is. The pack's theme: Budget choices combine empirical questions about effects with normative questions about who bears costs; define fairness before evaluating a reform.

Equal cash versus equal percentage: an equal cash cut takes a bigger share from those receiving less; an equal percentage cut takes more in pounds from those receiving more. Which is fairer depends on whether the student cares about burden relative to income or absolute loss, a good place to make them state their principle.

Incentives: cuts may encourage work if the barrier was the payment structure, but not if the barrier is illness, caring or lack of jobs. Second-round effects: hardship can raise costs elsewhere (health, housing).

The politics push-on tests party labels against coalitions: a right-wing party's voters may include pensioners or recipients seen as deserving; institutional commitments also matter.

Weak moves: asserting welfare is a right or a burden as if it settled the policy; ignoring who actually receives it; stopping at first-round savings.

**Sources:** [Political Currency, episode 333: Are the Tories against welfare cuts? (publisher description)](https://shows.acast.com/politicalcurrency-1/episodes/emqs-are-the-tories-against-welfare-cuts); Solomon's PPE interview pack (11 November 2026): original practice questions

## 8. Does democracy cause economic growth?

`democracy-and-growth` · economics · leads on to: testing-election-causes, resisting-education, climate-collective-action

**Prof Matt sees** (87 words)

Hook: rich countries tend to be democracies, but that does not tell us which way the arrow points. The tension: democracy might create growth, prosperity might create democracy, or something else might cause both. Push on: ask the student to define democracy and growth before comparing countries. How could we test whether democracy raises growth, and what would count as a credible comparison? If democracy raised GDP but worsened inequality, would it be economically successful? Is a peaceful transfer of power enough to make a system democratic?

**Background** (155 words)

From the What? Why? When? episode Does Democracy Cause Growth? (with Economics and PPE students), whose description highlights measurement, voting and transfers of power; and pack questions MWQ07, MWQ13 and MWQ14. The pack's exercise: build two causal stories (democracy creates growth; prosperity makes democracy more likely) then identify a possible common cause.

A strong student distinguishes association from causal mechanism. Possible mechanisms for democracy causing growth: secure property rights, accountability that punishes waste, investment in education. Reverse: richer, more educated populations demand democracy. Common causes: institutions, history, geography.

Testing: compare countries before and after transitions to democracy with similar countries that did not change; the student should see the problem of choosing a comparison group and why transitions are not random.

Definitions matter: is democracy elections, rights, or peaceful transfers? Is growth GDP or wellbeing? The GDP-versus-inequality push-on asks what economic success means.

Weak moves: citing rich democracies as proof; one-directional stories; not defining terms.

**Sources:** [What? Why? When? (Jesus College Oxford): Does Democracy Cause Growth? (publisher description)](https://shows.acast.com/what-why-when/episodes/680b6f0ad6473fbdddb5a02e); Solomon's PPE interview pack (11 November 2026): original practice questions

## 9. Is restricting new homes a fair way to reduce river pollution if other activities cause most of it?

`housing-water-pollution` · economics · leads on to: infrastructure-veto, lower-house-prices, rent-cap-day-after

**Prof Matt sees** (96 words)

Hook: in parts of England, rules to protect rivers from nutrient pollution have held up new housing, even though farming and old sewage systems may cause much of the pollution. The tension: new homes add a little pollution and are easy to regulate; the bigger sources are harder to tackle. Push on: should costs follow responsibility for pollution, ability to pay, or ease of regulation? One study found modest pollution gains alongside fewer homes and higher prices, with building moving next door. What comparison would show whether schemes that offset pollution elsewhere really improve water quality?

**Background** (196 words)

From Jan David Bakker and Niclas Moneke, "Combatting nutrient pollution through the housing market", working paper, 2026 (revise and resubmit at the Journal of Urban Economics; not an accepted article). In our own words: studying England's Nutrient Neutrality policy, which regulates new housing to protect water quality, they find modest pollution improvements alongside fewer completions, higher prices and construction shifting to neighbouring areas. Areas with offset schemes show better environmental outcomes and smaller housing losses; areas without them fare worse. Local administrative capacity and policy design are crucial to reconciling environmental protection and housing supply.

A strong student separates fairness (who should bear the cost: polluter, payer, or the easiest target) from effectiveness (does it reduce pollution?) and side effects (higher prices, displacement). Regulating the easy target is administratively convenient but may be unfair and inefficient.

Evidence for offsets: compare similar catchments with and without schemes, before and after, measuring water quality, not just permits issued.

Weak moves: assuming any environmental rule is good, or any rule blocking homes is bad; ignoring displacement; asserting cause from a before-and-after alone. The pack pairs this with the infrastructure-veto card. The student is not expected to know the paper.

**Sources:** [Combatting nutrient pollution through the housing market (working paper, 2026)](https://niclasmoneke.com/wp-content/uploads/Bakker_Moneke-Combatting_Nutrient_Pollution-2026.pdf); Solomon's PPE interview pack (11 November 2026): original practice questions
