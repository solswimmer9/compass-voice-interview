## 1. Can a river be a person?

`river-person` · human · leads on to: river-natural-or-human, regeneration-who-for, volcano-eruption-styles

**Dr River sees** (101 words)

Hook: in 2017 New Zealand recognised the Whanganui River as a legal person, reflecting the Māori view of the river as a living ancestor. The tension: there are two ways of knowing one river, and only one of them has the power to make the rules. Push on: what changes in practice, and who speaks for the river? Does writing this into the state's own law respect the Māori view or absorb it? Rules shape places and places shape rules: where else does a rule change what a place is, such as a park bylaw or a "no ball games" sign?

**Background** (168 words)

Underlying argument (Charpleix, on legal personhood and legal pluralism): the Whanganui settlement brings together two ways of knowing one river. In the Māori view the river is a living ancestor; in state law it is now a legal person. Only one of those systems has the power to make and enforce rules, so recognising the river in the state's own legal terms can be read either as respect for the Māori view or as absorbing it into the state's categories. A related idea from the notes (Delaney): law and space shape each other. Rules shape places, and places shape rules; school corridors, park bylaws and "no ball games" signs are everyday versions.

Questions that test the position: what actually changes in practice once the river is a legal person, and who speaks for it.

Weak moves to watch: treating the Māori community or the state as one uniform view; treating "natural" as politically neutral; retreating to a memorised case study; giving a both-sides summary without reaching a position.

**Sources:** Dr River question line: Can a river be a person?; Charpleix (2018), on the Whanganui River; Solomon's Part II geography notes: rules shape places

## 2. When a run-down area is regenerated, who is it really for?

`regeneration-who-for` · human · leads on to: local-area, river-person, volcano-eruption-styles

**Dr River sees** (74 words)

Hook: a smart new waterfront in a former docklands, or a new shopping centre or gated estate. The tension: who is a nice new place actually for? Push on: whose money paid for it, and was any of it public? Who is priced out, and where do they go? Can a pleasant place also be an exclusive one? Ask the student to start from a regenerated place they know, or from their own town.

**Background** (124 words)

Underlying argument (Harvey, on utopias that degenerate): the question is who a nice new place is actually for. Regenerated waterfronts, shopping centres and gated estates are the hooks. Public money may pay for a place that some of the public are priced out of.

The tension to hold the student on: whether a pleasant place can also be an exclusive one. A strong answer separates who pays, who benefits and who leaves.

Weak moves to watch: treating a community as one uniform thing, as if everyone in it gains or loses alike; assuming the problem must be solved at the same scale as its cause; a both-sides summary without reaching a position; conceding as soon as challenged, or repeating the same point more loudly.

**Sources:** Dr River question line: Who is a regenerated place for?; Spaces of Hope (2000)

## 3. Why do some volcanoes ooze and others explode?

`volcano-eruption-styles` · physical · leads on to: disaster-natural, hazards-city-volcano, regeneration-who-for, river-person

**Dr River sees** (89 words)

Hook: compare the gentle shield volcanoes of Hawaii, a steep cone like Mount St Helens, small cinder cones and huge calderas like Yellowstone. The tension: the same basic process, molten rock reaching the surface, behaves very differently. Push on: what do the magma's stickiness and gas do? How does the plate setting shape this? Is the most explosive type also the most deadly? Do neat categories fit a real volcano? Reason from first principles, and if it fits, ask why anyone would build a city next to a volcano.

**Background** (149 words)

Underlying argument: eruption style depends on the magma's stickiness and its gas, and the plate setting shapes both. Compare shield volcanoes like those of Hawaii, steep composite cones like Mount St Helens, small cinder cones and huge calderas like Yellowstone. A strong candidate reasons this through from first principles rather than reciting a table of volcano types.

Positions to test: whether the most explosive type is also the most deadly, which turns on who is hit hardest; whether neat categories fit a real volcano, since categories are things people made.

Link to human geography: why would anyone build a city next to a volcano? Use it to show how physical geography matters to human geography.

Weak moves to watch: naming a type instead of explaining the process; treating "natural" as politically neutral when discussing who is harmed; keeping physical and human processes separate; retreating to a memorised case study.

**Sources:** Dr River question line: Why do some volcanoes ooze and others explode?

## 15. Are trees more valuable than gold?

`trees-or-gold` · human · leads on to: outdoors-ownership, ai-climate, trees-climate-solution

**Dr River sees** (116 words)

Hook: gold is prized largely because it is scarce, while a living tree gives shade, holds soil, stores carbon and shelters other species, yet often earns money only once it is cut down. The tension: price and value are not the same thing, and someone decides which one counts. Push on: valuable to whom, a logger, a city in a heatwave, a family downstream of a cleared hillside? Why can a standing tree earn nothing while a felled one earns money? Does putting a price on a forest protect it or make it easier to trade away? Does the answer change between one street tree and a whole forest, or between next year and next century?

**Background** (84 words)

The question turns on what "valuable" means and who gets to say. A strong answer separates price (what something sells for) from value (what depends on it), notices that the same tree is worth different things to different people and at different scales, and sees that markets tend to pay for what can be removed and sold.

Weak moves to watch: treating "valuable" as having one obvious meaning; treating nature as politically neutral; a both-sides summary without a position; conceding as soon as challenged.

**Sources:** Owner's question: trees are more valuable than gold

## 16. What do you think about mining the moon?

`mining-the-moon` · human · leads on to: outdoors-ownership, trees-or-gold, geopolitics-arctic

**Dr River sees** (124 words)

Hook: the Moon has water ice near its poles and useful metals, and several countries and companies plan to extract them. A 1967 treaty says no country can claim the Moon as its territory, but it is much less clear who owns what is dug out. The tension: this is a place nobody owns and nobody lives in, so do the usual arguments about mining still apply? Push on: who can get there first, and who is left out? If no people or wildlife are harmed, is anything wrong with it? Is it like the deep sea or Antarctica, and what did we decide there? Would it ease pressure on the Earth or just carry the same habits further? Who should write the rules?

**Background** (94 words)

The question tests whether the student can reason about ownership, access and rules in a place with no residents and no borders. A strong answer asks who benefits and who is excluded, compares it with other places treated as shared (the deep sea, Antarctica), and separates "is it harmful?" from "is it fair?".

Weak moves to watch: assuming an empty place raises no questions of fairness; assuming a problem must be solved at the same scale as its cause; treating "countries" as single actors when companies are involved; a both-sides summary without a position.

**Sources:** Owner's question: mining the moon
