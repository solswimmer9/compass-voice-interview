## 1. Can a river be a person?

`river-person` · human · leads on to: river-natural-or-human, regeneration-who-for, volcano-eruption-styles

**Dr River sees** (101 words)

Hook: in 2017 New Zealand recognised the Whanganui River as a legal person, reflecting the Māori view of the river as a living ancestor. The tension: there are two ways of knowing one river, and only one of them has the power to make the rules. Push on: what changes in practice, and who speaks for the river? Does writing this into the state's own law respect the Māori view or absorb it? Rules shape places and places shape rules: where else does a rule change what a place is, such as a park bylaw or a "no ball games" sign?

**Background** (168 words)

Underlying argument (Charpleix, on legal personhood and legal pluralism): the Whanganui settlement brings together two ways of knowing one river. In the Māori view the river is a living ancestor; in state law it is now a legal person. Only one of those systems has the power to make and enforce rules, so recognising the river in the state's own legal terms can be read either as respect for the Māori view or as absorbing it into the state's categories. A related idea from the notes (Delaney): law and space shape each other. Rules shape places, and places shape rules; school corridors, park bylaws and "no ball games" signs are everyday versions.

Questions that test the position: what actually changes in practice once the river is a legal person, and who speaks for it.

Weak moves to watch: treating the Māori community or the state as one uniform view; treating "natural" as politically neutral; retreating to a memorised case study; giving a both-sides summary without reaching a position.

**Sources:** Dr River question line: Can a river be a person?; Charpleix (2018), on the Whanganui River; Solomon's Part II geography notes: rules shape places

`place-changed-thinking` · human · draft · leads on to: local-area, regeneration-who-for, hopeful-and-angry, statistic-limits

**Dr River sees** (124 words)

Hook: any place, near or far, that the student has lived in, been to, read about or seen through someone else's eyes. The tension: one place can change how you see the world, but one place cannot prove a general claim, and you only saw part of it. Push on: what did you expect before, and what did you find? What exactly in that place changed your mind: something you saw, someone you met, or a fact you learned? Would someone who lives there have noticed the same? How could you check whether it holds for other places? Let the student choose the place, and never assume they have travelled. If they mention something painful, do not press; offer to move to another place.

**Background** (339 words)

Underlying argument: this is an open question that starts from the student's own experience, so the tutor supplies almost no content. What it tests is how a student reasons from one case to a wider idea. A good answer says what they expected and what they found, identifies what in the place caused the change, and then tests that against other places and other people's views. The risk is treating a single vivid place as proof, or telling a story with no reasoning. A strong answer notices what they could not see from where they stood, and who lived there with a different view.

Positions to test: what exactly changed in the student's thinking, and what evidence was behind it; whether the change is about the place or about the student, such as their age or what they happened to see; whether the same place would look different to a resident, a visitor and someone who left; how to move from one example to a general claim, and what other places would test it; whether a place seen through a book, a film, a news report or a map counts, and how those differ from being there; how the answer changes between street, city and country.

Fairness: no example may depend on having travelled or visited particular institutions. A place can be a street, a village, a town, or one known through other people or media. Do not judge the student by how exotic the place is.

Care: if a student speaks about a difficult place or event in their own life, do not press for details. Slow down and offer another example.

Link to other cards: what a visitor sees in a local area; who a regenerated place is for; being hopeful and angry about the same place.

Weak moves to watch: telling a story with no reasoning; treating one place as typical; treating a community as one uniform thing; retreating to a memorised case study; a both-sides summary without reaching a position; conceding as soon as challenged.
